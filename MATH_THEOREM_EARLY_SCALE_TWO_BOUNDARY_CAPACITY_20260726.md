# Early-scale component routing has exactly two boundary bits

Date: 2026-07-26

This note audits the alternative in which one intervenes at the first
Catalan scale comparable with the row-power capacity.  Let

\[
 s=\min\{t:\operatorname {Cat}_t\ge p\},
 \qquad d=\operatorname {Cat}_s.
 \tag{0.1}
\]

Then

\[
             \operatorname {Cat}_{s-1}<p\le d<4p.      \tag{0.2}
\]

Thus four output targets have enough scalar capacity for one canonical
size-\(s\) fibre.  The question is whether ancestor substitutions really
supply four distinct labels and whether the full crossing-window profile,
not only the distinguished nested window, respects those four bins.

The exact answer is:

* in an influence-separated atlas, every consecutive window sees at most
  two routing bits, its left and right boundary bits;
* two separated boundary swaps give a literal Boolean square of four
  distinct targets, so there is no statewise capacity obstruction at
  (0.2);
* if only one bit is visible, a statewise obstruction survives whenever
  \(d>2p\);
* for one complete early-scale family of elementary MSW rectangles, the
  positive crossing-window collision inside one parent atom is already
  below \(p\), even without exterior labels;
* the canonical Klein-four overlay generated by the two endpoint
  transpositions does **not** balance the four Boolean cells: its principal
  no-interior-return block is one ownership component, and selecting any
  of its four shores merely permutes the load vector
  \((C_s,C_{s-1},C_{s-1},C_{s-2})\).

Thus the early-scale numerical capacity survives, but the canonical
coordinate-overlay implementation does not.  Any surviving implementation
must fragment the no-interior-return block by a genuinely non-coordinate
packet or trade.

## 1. Boundary locality in an influence-separated nesting

Consider one elementary rooted rectangle.  In either affected row, write
the unchanged neighbours, old state and new state as

\[
                         L, M, M', R.
\]

The octahedral identity is

\[
                         L\cap M\cap R
                       = L\cap M'\cap R.              \tag{1.1}
\]

### Theorem 1.1 (two-boundary dependence for a separated atlas)

Let \(I=[a,a+q]\) be any consecutive intersection window in a row which
contains a collection of elementary rectangle switches.  Assume every
switched phase strictly inside \(I\) has its two immediate canonical
neighbour states unchanged; in particular this holds when all switched
phases are pairwise nonadjacent.  Then the target of \(I\) can depend only
on switches at phases \(a\) and \(a+q\).
Consequently one window has at most four target values under all choices
of the installed rectangles.

#### Proof

If a switched phase \(h\) lies strictly between \(a\) and \(a+q\), then
the window contains \(L,M,R\) before the switch and \(L,M',R\) after it.
Equation (1.1) makes their contribution to the total intersection equal.
A phase outside the window is irrelevant.  Only \(h=a\) or \(h=a+q\)
can change the intersection.  Each boundary has two states, giving at
most \(2^2=4\) values. \(\square\)

The separation hypothesis is essential.  Arbitrary nested substitutions
can put switched phases next to one another.  The triple identity for one
switch then uses a neighbour altered by the other switch, and an interior
adjacent cluster can affect the target jointly.  Such a cluster must be
treated as one joint atom; Theorem 1.1 does not reduce it to its two outer
window boundaries.  Thus six *separated* stages give at most two effective
bits to one window, whereas six arbitrary nested stages require a new
cluster-profile theorem.

## 2. When the four values are genuinely distinct

For a boundary rectangle, its isolated target action has the form

\[
             \partial_{\beta,\gamma}K
             =e_{K\cup\{\gamma\}}-e_{K\cup\{\beta\}}.
 \tag{2.1}
\]

Suppose the two boundary phases of one window carry swaps on disjoint
coordinate pairs

\[
 \{\beta_L,\gamma_L\}\cap
 \{\beta_R,\gamma_R\}=\varnothing,                    \tag{2.2}
\]

and every one of these four coordinates survives all the states between
the two boundaries in exactly the indicated endpoint slot.  Then for a
fixed common core \(K\), the four target values are

\[
 T_{\epsilon,\eta}
 =K\cup\{x_L(\epsilon),x_R(\eta)\},
 \quad
 x_L(0)=\beta_L, x_L(1)=\gamma_L,
 \quad
 x_R(0)=\beta_R, x_R(1)=\gamma_R.                    \tag{2.3}
\]

### Lemma 2.1 (Boolean-square collision criterion)

Under (2.2)--(2.3), the four sets \(T_{00},T_{01},T_{10},T_{11}\) are
pairwise distinct.  Adjoining any one common exterior set \(E\), disjoint
from the four displayed local coordinates, preserves their distinctness.

#### Proof

Intersection with the left coordinate pair recovers \(\epsilon\), and
intersection with the right pair recovers \(\eta\).  A common exterior
union changes neither recovery. \(\square\)

Thus ancestor material can indeed encode a second label: an exterior
boundary swap on a coordinate pair disjoint from the local pair turns the
old two-cell orbit into a four-cell product orbit.  This is the precise
way in which an ancestor context can escape the local
\(\{2,3\}\)-orbit floor.

There is also a sharp converse at the level of capacity.

### Corollary 2.2 (one-bit statewise no-go)

If a fibre of \(d\) occurrences sees at most one nontrivial boundary bit,
then all its images lie in at most two physical targets.  Hence some target
has load at least \(d/2\).  In particular cap \(p\) is impossible whenever
\(d>2p\).

At the early scale (0.2), the four-cell case has no corresponding scalar
obstruction, because \(d/4<p\).  Therefore every source fibre with
\(d>2p\) must be certified to see two **independent** boundary pairs; one
visible boundary is not enough.

## 3. The exact four-arm profile and its early-scale collision bound

For one elementary MSW rectangle, rotate its omitted-label word so that
the four exceptional letters come first, and split the common tail into
the parity lists \(\mathsf E,\mathsf O\).  At rank \(m-q\), put
\(\ell=m-q-1\).  Its full signed target vector is

\[
\begin{aligned}
 d_q={}&
   \partial\operatorname {suf}_{\ell}(\mathsf O)
  +\partial\operatorname {suf}_{\ell}(\mathsf E)\\
 &-\partial\operatorname {pre}_{\ell}(\mathsf E)
  -\partial\operatorname {pre}_{\ell}(\mathsf O).
\end{aligned}                                         \tag{3.1}
\]

This is the complete crossing-window profile: two suffix arms, two prefix
arms, and no hidden interior term.

Now use inside one size-\((s+1)\) parent all elementary rectangles indexed
by \(R\in\mathcal D_{s-1}\).  Put

\[
                         c=\operatorname {Cat}_{s-1}. \tag{3.2}
\]

For a fixed parent context, summing (3.1) gives

\[
 Z_C=c\,\partial_C(A_C+B_C)
       -\sum_{R\in\mathcal D_{s-1}}
          \partial_C(P^E_{C,R}+P^O_{C,R}),             \tag{3.3}
\]

where the two suffix cores \(A_C,B_C\) are fixed and the two prefix cores
carry the filling \(R\).  Formula (3.3), rather than the distinguished
child target alone, is the full one-parent ledger.

For the still earlier size-\(s\) rectangle layer, the number of rectangles
is \(c_-=\operatorname {Cat}_{s-2}\).  Minimality in (0.1) gives

\[
                       2c_-\le\operatorname {Cat}_{s-1}<p
 \tag{3.4}
\]

for all \(s\ge3\) (with equality in the first inequality only at the
small base case).

### Theorem 3.1 (one-atom full-profile safety at the early scale)

Fix one aligned parent context and one protected depth.  Apply the complete
size-\(s\) elementary-rectangle family inside that parent.  For any one
physical target, the total number of **new positive crossing-window
occurrences** created by the whole family is less than \(p\).

The same remains true for a window meeting two nested early-scale
rectangle layers: its two boundary phases can contribute at most
\(2c_-<p\) new occurrences to one target.

#### Proof

By Theorem 1.1 a fixed window can charge a switch only at one of its two
boundaries.  At one boundary, at most all \(c_-\) row-disjoint rectangles
in the parent can contribute to the same target.  A window has two
boundaries, so the worst possible positive collision is \(2c_-\), which
is below \(p\) by (3.4).  Formula (3.1) confirms that these are all four
arms: there is no interior contribution omitted by the boundary count.
\(\square\)

This is a genuine advantage of intervening one Catalan scale earlier.
At the fatal scale the fixed arms themselves exceed \(p\); at (0.1), even
complete collision of both visible boundary arms fits below the cap.

## 4. What is and is not proved bottom-up

Within one fixed ancestor context, a common exterior set cannot merge the
four local values of Lemma 2.1.  Therefore a two-boundary component library
with cell counts

\[
                         n_{00},n_{01},n_{10},n_{11}   \tag{4.1}
\]

would solve the local capacity problem exactly if

\[
                         \max n_{\epsilon,\eta}\le p. \tag{4.2}
\]

Condition (0.2) makes (4.2) arithmetically possible.  The complete
crossing-window profile of each independently rounded early-scale atom is
also pointwise safe by Theorem 3.1.

Two further statements would be required for a global theorem.

1. **Balanced four-cell realizability.**  Exact ownership-component choices
   must realize (4.2) simultaneously at both window boundaries.  The
   one-coordinate Catalan hierarchy gives only one marginal split; its
   component partition is not laminar with the partition at the opposite
   boundary, even in \(D_3\).
2. **Cross-context separation.**  Lemma 2.1 prevents collisions among the
   four labels inside one common exterior.  It does not prevent a target
   produced in one aligned parent context from equalling a target produced
   in another context with different exterior material.  One needs either
   a context marker, an injective exterior-label theorem, or a joint-atom
   residual-capacity argument.

Sections 5--7 settle the first statement negatively for the canonical
Klein-four coordinate overlay.  Thus an influence-separated atlas really
does enlarge the physical target set from two cells to four, but the four
loads are not independently selectable.  Any surviving bottom-up theorem
must use a non-coordinate packet which changes that ownership-component
geometry; it must then also solve the cross-context condition in item 2.

## 5. Finite-group ownership switching

The correct way to test the proposed four-cell routing is to overlay all
four exact factors at once.  The following lemma works for an arbitrary
finite family, not only for a Klein four group.

### Lemma 5.1 (finite-group component switching)

Let \(G\) be a finite group acting on the middle vertices \(\Omega\), and
let \(F_g\), \(g\in G\), be exact row factors of \(\Omega\), every row
having the same size \(n\).  Form the \(|G|\)-partite ownership
hypergraph whose vertices in part \(g\) are the rows of \(F_g\), and whose
hyperedge labelled \(x\in\Omega\) joins the unique owner of \(x\) in
each part.

For every connected component \(K\):

1. the rows of every part in \(K\) partition the same label set
   \(\Omega_K\);
2. every part contains exactly \(|\Omega_K|/n\) rows in \(K\);
3. independently for each component, replacing its rows by all the rows
   from any one chosen part produces another exact factor.

If, in addition, all \(F_g\) have been re-anchored so that the owner of
the distinguished root token \(D\) has port \(D\), then the set of ports
occurring in \(K\) is the same in every part.  Hence the switch also
preserves port transversality.

#### Proof

If a row vertex belongs to \(K\), all \(n\) ownership hyperedges incident
with it belong to \(K\).  Conversely every hyperedge in \(K\) contains
one vertex in every part.  It follows that, in each part, the row supports
are disjoint and have union exactly \(\Omega_K\).  Counting their labels
gives \(n|F_g\cap K|=|\Omega_K|\), independently of \(g\).  The label
sets \(\Omega_K\) partition \(\Omega\), so choosing one whole shore in
each component partitions \(\Omega\) again.

For the anchored refinement, the hyperedge labelled by a root token
\(D\) joins, in every part, the row with port \(D\).  Root-token
hyperedges therefore identify the same port set on every shore of a
component. \(\square\)

This lemma also covers coincident rows and coloured copies: rows are
vertices in their factor parts, while equality of their underlying
supports merely creates parallel incidences in the ownership description.

## 6. The canonical Klein-four component

Let the parent factor be \(F_{s+1}\), with roots in
\(\mathcal D_{s+1}\).  Let \(L\) be the left endpoint transposition and
\(R\) its right-end mirror.  They commute, so

\[
                         V_4=\langle L,R\rangle
                         =\{1,L,R,LR\}.              \tag{6.1}
\]

Write a Dyck word as a concatenation of primitive excursions and record
the return cuts by their semilength positions.  Both endpoint moves
preserve every return cut in positions \(2,\ldots,s-1\).  The block with
no such interior return cut is

\[
\begin{aligned}
 \mathcal B_s={}&
 \{\,1u0:u\in\mathcal D_s\,\}\\
 &\mathbin{\dot\cup}
 \{\,10\,1v0:v\in\mathcal D_{s-1}\,\}\\
 &\mathbin{\dot\cup}
 \{\,1v0\,10:v\in\mathcal D_{s-1}\,\}\\
 &\mathbin{\dot\cup}
 \{\,10\,1w0\,10:w\in\mathcal D_{s-2}\,\}.
                                                               \tag{6.2}
\end{aligned}
\]

Thus its four family sizes are

\[
 (d,e,e,f)
 =\bigl(C_s,C_{s-1},C_{s-1},C_{s-2}\bigr).          \tag{6.3}
\]

### Theorem 6.1 (no-interior-return component)

The roots in \(\mathcal B_s\) form one connected component of the
four-factor ownership overlay of

\[
                         F_{s+1},\quad LF_{s+1},
                         \quad RF_{s+1},\quad LRF_{s+1}.         \tag{6.4}
\]

No root with an interior return cut belongs to this component.

#### Proof

The exact one-end component theorem for \(L\), taken at its largest
primitive level, joins

\[
 \{1u0:u\in\mathcal D_s\}
 \quad\hbox{to}\quad
 \{10\,1v0:v\in\mathcal D_{s-1}\}.                  \tag{6.5}
\]

Its right-end mirror joins the first family to
\(\{1v0\,10:v\in\mathcal D_{s-1}\}\).  Applying the right-end relation
after the left-end relation joins the latter left-leaf family to
\(\{10\,1w0\,10:w\in\mathcal D_{s-2}\}\).  Hence all four families in
(6.2) lie in one component of the join of the two ownership relations.

Conversely, an endpoint relation changes only the optional leaf at its
own end and preserves all ground-return cuts in positions
\(2,\ldots,s-1\).  Therefore the component cannot leave \(\mathcal B_s\).
The four-factor ownership connectivity is precisely the join of the
left- and right-overlay connectivity relations, proving the claim.
\(\square\)

There is a useful complete refinement.  If the fixed interior return cuts
are nonempty, with first cut \(a\) and last cut \(b\), then the corresponding
component factors into independent left and right end families.  Its row
count is

\[
                         L_aL_{s+1-b},
 \qquad L_h=C_{h-1}+C_{h-2}.                         \tag{6.6}
\]

The no-cut component (6.2) is the extremal member and is the only one
needed for the obstruction below.

## 7. Exact action on the four Boolean cells

Use the two disjoint endpoint coordinate pairs from (2.2), and let
\(T_{00},T_{10},T_{01},T_{11}\) be the Boolean-square targets (2.3).
For the distinguished parent window, the four root families in (6.2)
give respectively

\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
 \text{root type}&\text{target}&\text{multiplicity}\\ \hline
 1u0&T_{00}&C_s=d\\
 10\,1v0&T_{10}&C_{s-1}=e\\
 1v0\,10&T_{01}&C_{s-1}=e\\
 10\,1w0\,10&T_{11}&C_{s-2}=f.
\end{array}                                           \tag{7.1}
\]

The endpoint moves toggle the corresponding Boolean coordinate.  Hence
the four shores of the component do not realize arbitrary redistributions:
they only permute the vector

\[
                              (d,e,e,f).              \tag{7.2}
\]

### Corollary 7.1 (canonical Klein-four no-go)

Every exact factor obtained by independently choosing shores of the
canonical \(V_4\) ownership components has, on the four cells of the
principal component, maximum load at least

\[
                              d=C_s.                  \tag{7.3}
\]

Consequently, whenever \(C_s>p\), canonical two-end coordinate-component
switching cannot enforce the cap \(\max T_{\epsilon,\eta}\le p\), even
inside one parent context.  Adjoining a common ancestor exterior does not
alter this conclusion.

#### Proof

By Lemma 5.1 a legal component choice selects one complete shore.  By
(7.1)--(7.2), every shore places the entry \(d\) in one of the four cells.
Loads are nonnegative, so contributions from other components cannot
remove it.  A common exterior adjoins the same set to all four cells and
preserves both their identities and their multiplicities. \(\square\)

Even if the component were divisible and its mass could be redistributed
arbitrarily, its total gives a second necessary condition.  Put

\[
 M_s=d+2e+f=\Lambda_s d,
 \qquad
 \Lambda_s
 =\frac{5s(5s-7)}{4(2s-1)(2s-3)}
 \longrightarrow\frac{25}{16}.                       \tag{7.4}
\]

Four bins of capacity \(p\) require

\[
 \frac d p\le\frac4{\Lambda_s}
 \longrightarrow\frac{64}{25}=2.56.                 \tag{7.5}
\]

Thus \(C_s<4p\) was not, by itself, the correct scalar ledger for the
whole component.  More importantly, component indivisibility strengthens
(7.5) to the statewise obstruction (7.3).

The early-scale route therefore has one sharply delimited survivor:

> Find a **non-coordinate** two-boundary packet or trade which fragments
> the no-interior-return block \(\mathcal B_s\), while retaining the full
> prefix/suffix safety of (3.1) and separating different ancestor
> contexts.

The explicit four-cell capacity is real, but the canonical Klein-four
coordinate overlay cannot access it.
