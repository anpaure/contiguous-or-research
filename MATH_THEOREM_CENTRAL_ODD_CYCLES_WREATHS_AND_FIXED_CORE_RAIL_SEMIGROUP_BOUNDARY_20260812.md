# Central odd cycles are wreaths, but fixed-core rail factors have an extra positive-semigroup divisibility

**Date:** 2026-08-12
**Method:** odd-cycle membership words, exact phase decomposition of a
cyclic-window deck, and point-degree divisibility
**Status:** unconditional theorem and citation-safe literature boundary. It
proves the exact equivalence between central odd-graph cycles and central
wreaths, and an actual positive-semigroup obstruction for every fixed-core
pure-rail factor when a point divisibility fails. It does **not** obstruct a
global rail factor whose components use overlapping, varying cores.

## 1. Conventions

For a finite set \(X\), \(|X|=N\), a cyclic order

\[
 \sigma=(x_0,x_1,\ldots,x_{N-1})
\]

and \(1\le q<N\), put

\[
 \mathcal D_q(\sigma)
 =\bigl\{\{x_i,x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{i+q-1}\}:i\in\mathbb Z_N\bigr\}.
\tag{1.1}
\]

This is the **all-start cyclic \(q\)-window deck**. Its \(N\) sets are
distinct.

The Baranyai--Katona \((N,q)\)-wreath generated by \(\sigma\) uses starts
separated by \(q\):

\[
 \mathcal W_q(\sigma)
 =\bigl\{\{x_{jq},x_{jq+1},\ldots,x_{jq+q-1}\}:
          j\in\mathbb Z_{N/g}\bigr\},
 \qquad g=\gcd(N,q).
\tag{1.2}
\]

Thus a wreath has \(N/g\) sets. Formula (1.2) is the convention in
Petr--Turek's formulation of Baranyai's wreath conjecture. When \(g=1\),
(1.1) and (1.2) are the same set family, merely enumerated in different
orders. When \(g>1\), they are not the same object.

## 2. Every minimum odd-graph cycle is a wreath

Let \(O_{2q+1}=KG(2q+1,q)\) be the odd graph: its vertices are the
\(q\)-subsets of a \((2q+1)\)-set \(X\), with two vertices adjacent when
they are disjoint.

### Theorem 2.1 (minimum odd cycle if and only if central wreath)

A family of \(2q+1\) distinct \(q\)-sets is the vertex set of a
\(C_{2q+1}\) in \(O_{2q+1}\) if and only if it is a
\((2q+1,q)\)-wreath.

#### Proof

First take a cycle

\[
 A_0,A_1,\ldots,A_{2q},A_0
\tag{2.1}
\]

in the odd graph. Since adjacent \(q\)-sets are disjoint, for every
\(i\in\mathbb Z_{2q+1}\) there is one point

\[
 x_i\in X\setminus(A_i\cup A_{i+1}).
\tag{2.2}
\]

Fix \(x\in X\), and write

\[
 b_i(x)=\mathbf 1_{x\in A_i}.
\]

The cyclic binary word \(b_0(x)\cdots b_{2q}(x)\) has no adjacent ones.
If \(w_x=\sum_i b_i(x)\), then the number \(z_x\) of cyclic \(00\)-pairs
in this word is

\[
 z_x=(2q+1)-2w_x.
\tag{2.3}
\]

Indeed, every one is incident with exactly two non-\(00\) pairs, and no
pair is incident with two ones. Since \(w_x\le q\), equation (2.3) says
that \(z_x\) is a positive odd integer. On the other hand, \(z_x\) is
exactly the number of indices \(i\) for which the missing point in (2.2)
is \(x\). Consequently

\[
 \sum_{x\in X}z_x=2q+1.
\]

There are \(2q+1\) summands and every one is at least one, so

\[
 z_x=1\qquad(x\in X).
\tag{2.4}
\]

In particular, \(i\mapsto x_i\) is a bijection from
\(\mathbb Z_{2q+1}\) to \(X\).

For \(x=x_j\), its unique \(00\)-pair is at \(j,j+1\). Away from that
pair, the membership word has neither \(00\) nor \(11\), and hence it
alternates. Therefore

\[
 x_j\in A_i
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 i-j\in\{2,4,\ldots,2q\}\pmod{2q+1}.
\tag{2.5}
\]

As multiplication by two permutes \(\mathbb Z_{2q+1}\), define the cyclic
order \(y_s=x_{2s}\). Equation (2.5) gives, for every \(s\),

\[
 A_{2s}=\{y_{s-1},y_{s-2},\ldots,y_{s-q}\}.
\tag{2.6}
\]

As \(2s\) runs through all indices, (2.6) is exactly the family of all
cyclic \(q\)-windows of \(y\). Since \(\gcd(2q+1,q)=1\), this family is
the wreath generated by \(y\).

Conversely, let

\[
 B_s=\{y_s,y_{s+1},\ldots,y_{s+q-1}\}
 \qquad(s\in\mathbb Z_{2q+1})
\]

be a central wreath. The two windows \(B_s\) and \(B_{s+q}\) are
disjoint. Since addition by \(q\) is a cyclic permutation of
\(\mathbb Z_{2q+1}\), the order

\[
 B_0,B_q,B_{2q},\ldots
\]

is a \(C_{2q+1}\) in \(O_{2q+1}\). This proves both directions.
\(\square\)

### Corollary 2.2 (the central wreath decomposition is a theorem)

M\"utze--Standke--Wiechert prove that \(O_{2q+1}\) has a
\(C_{2q+1}\)-factor consisting of

\[
 \operatorname{Cat}_q
 =\frac1{q+1}{2q\choose q}
 =\frac1{2q+1}{2q+1\choose q}
\]

cycles. By Theorem 2.1, the vertex family of every one of those cycles is
a \((2q+1,q)\)-wreath. Their cycle factor therefore decomposes

\[
 {X\choose q}
\]

into central wreaths.

This corollary concerns the unrestricted owner deck. For \(q\ge2\), a
later exact residence audit corrected the catalogue boundary: period
\(N=2q+1\) is already a legal biresident pure rail. Its toggle traces are
cyclic shifts of \(1^q0^{q+1}\), so both run lengths meet the depth-\(d\)
threshold \(D=d+1=q\); its immediate palettes and every proper interval
row are simple. Thus, in that range, the factor above is an exact factor
by legal resident rails, not merely an unrestricted owner factor. The stronger hypothesis
\(N\ge2q+2\) remains useful for later local ports requiring two exterior
toggle labels, but is not a residence axiom.  See
`MATH_THEOREM_PERIOD_2Q_PLUS_1_PURE_RAIL_RESIDENCE_AND_CENTRAL_WREATH_FACTOR_20260813.md`.

## 3. Exact relation between maximal pure rails and wreaths

Fix a pure-rail core \(C\) of size \(c=R-q\), and put

\[
 X=[k]\setminus C,\qquad |X|=M=k-c.
\tag{3.1}
\]

Owners containing \(C\) are in bijection with the \(q\)-subsets of \(X\),
via

\[
 Q\longmapsto C\cup Q.
\tag{3.2}
\]

A period-\(N\) pure rail with this core chooses

\[
 T\in{X\choose N},\qquad 2q\le N\le M,
\tag{3.3}
\]

and contributes, under (3.2), the all-start deck
\(\mathcal D_q(\sigma)\) of a cyclic order on \(T\).

### Lemma 3.1 (phase decomposition)

Put \(g=\gcd(N,q)\). Every period-\(N\) all-start deck is the disjoint
union of \(g\) \((N,q)\)-wreaths sharing one cyclic order:

\[
 \mathcal D_q(\sigma)
 =\mathop{\dot\bigcup}_{r=0}^{g-1}
 \bigl\{I^q_{r+jq}(\sigma):j\in\mathbb Z_{N/g}\bigr\}.
\tag{3.4}
\]

#### Proof

Addition by \(q\) on \(\mathbb Z_N\) has exactly \(g\) orbits, the cosets
of the subgroup generated by \(q\), each of size \(N/g\). Restricting
the starts in (1.1) to one orbit gives the wreath generated by the
corresponding rotation of \(\sigma\). The \(g\) start orbits partition
\(\mathbb Z_N\), and distinct starts give distinct proper windows.
\(\square\)

For maximal periods \(N=M\), Lemma 3.1 has the following exact
consequences.

1. If \(\gcd(M,q)=1\), a fixed-core maximal pure rail is exactly a
   Baranyai--Katona \((M,q)\)-wreath. A decomposition of the whole fixed
   core fibre into maximal rails is therefore exactly the wreath
   conjecture for \((M,q)\).
2. If \(g=\gcd(M,q)>1\), a maximal rail is a bundle of \(g\) companion
   wreaths coming from the same cyclic order. A maximal-rail decomposition
   implies a wreath decomposition after splitting every rail, but the
   converse additionally requires the wreaths to group into these
   common-order \(g\)-tuples.
3. Allowing proper supports \(T\subsetneq X\) and the full period range
   (3.3) gives a different, more flexible **mixed-support** decomposition
   problem. It is neither the statement nor a consequence of the
   \((M,q)\)-wreath conjecture, because its blocks are wreath bundles on
   smaller ground sets \(T\).

## 4. An actual fixed-core positive-semigroup obstruction

The variable period range removes the elementary gcd of the block sizes,
but it does not remove the following point-degree obstruction.

### Theorem 4.1 (fixed-core point divisibility)

If the complete fixed-core owner fibre

\[
 \{C\cup Q:Q\in{X\choose q}\}
\tag{4.1}
\]

can be partitioned into pure rails of arbitrary unprotected resident periods
\(2q\le N\le M\), then necessarily

\[
 \boxed{q\mid{M-1\choose q-1}.}
\tag{4.2}
\]

#### Proof

In one period-\(N\) rail, a toggle point \(x\in T\) belongs to exactly
\(q\) of the \(N\) cyclic \(q\)-windows, while a point outside \(T\)
belongs to none. Hence every nonnegative sum of fixed-core rail columns
has \(q\)-divisible degree at each point of \(X\).

In the complete \(q\)-uniform layer on \(X\), every point has degree

\[
 {M-1\choose q-1}.
\]

Equality of the two incidence vectors forces (4.2).
\(\square\)

This is a positive-semigroup obstruction for the actual rail columns,
not a generic parity gadget. It disappears in the signed lattice only
because changing the core role introduces additional point currents.

### Corollary 4.2 (the \(k=17\) fixed-core fibre is impossible)

For \(k=17\),

\[
 R=9,\qquad d=3,\qquad q=4,\qquad c=5,\qquad M=12.
\]

The unprotected resident periods are \(8,9,10,11,12\), but

\[
 {M-1\choose q-1}={11\choose3}=165\not\equiv0\pmod4.
\]

Therefore no nonnegative collection of fixed-core pure rails of any legal
period can partition that core's complete owner fibre.

This does **not** contradict the full named-owner signed-lattice theorem,
and it does not obstruct a positive factor which mixes different cores.
It proves that a proof based on independently decomposing complete core
fibres cannot work, already at \(k=17\).

## 5. Why Baranyai 1-factorization cannot simply be sequenced

Suppose \(q\mid M\). Baranyai's factorization theorem decomposes
\({X\choose q}\) into

\[
 F={M-1\choose q-1}
\tag{5.1}
\]

perfect matchings. In this divisibility regime an \((M,q)\)-wreath is
exactly a perfect matching: its consecutive \(q\)-blocks partition \(X\),
and conversely the blocks of any perfect matching can be listed
consecutively in a permutation.

A maximal all-start rail is much more structured. By Lemma 3.1 it is a
bundle of \(q\) perfect matchings, namely the \(q\) start classes modulo
\(q\), all induced by one cyclic order. Thus turning a Baranyai
1-factorization into maximal rails requires at least

\[
 q\mid F={M-1\choose q-1},
\tag{5.2}
\]

and, beyond (5.2), a grouping of every \(q\) factors into the phase classes
of one common cyclic order. Baranyai's theorem supplies neither assertion.

At \((M,q)=(12,4)\), Baranyai gives \(165\) perfect matchings, but a
period-\(12\) rail would consume them four at a time. The failure
\(4\nmid165\) proves that no cyclic sequencing or regrouping exists. The
same divisibility also rules out rescue by the nonmaximal legal periods,
by Theorem 4.1.

For maximal rails, (5.2) is equivalently the scalar block condition

\[
 M\mid{M\choose q},
\]

because

\[
 {M\choose q}=\frac Mq{M-1\choose q-1}.
\]

The point-degree proof in Theorem 4.1 is stronger in scope: it survives
the admission of all nonmaximal periods.

## 6. Correct literature boundary

The following statements are safe to cite.

1. **Baranyai (1974).** If \(q\mid M\), the complete \(q\)-uniform
   hypergraph on \(M\) points decomposes into perfect matchings. In this
   case this proves the Baranyai--Katona wreath conjecture, but not a
   decomposition into all-start maximal rail decks.
2. **M\"utze--Standke--Wiechert (2018).** The odd graph \(O_{2q+1}\)
   has a \(C_{2q+1}\)-factor. Theorem 2.1 converts this into the central
   \((2q+1,q)\)-wreath decomposition.
3. **Petr--Turek (2025).** The general wreath conjecture asks for a
   wreath decomposition for every \((M,q)\). Their wreath-matrix paper
   gives exact algebraic equivalences and spectral information, but states
   that the general conjecture remains widely open, with only a few
   families known even at \(q=3\). Their companion Dyck-path paper poses
   stronger structured versions in the central case; those stronger
   conjectures do not negate the unstructured central decomposition in
   Corollary 2.2.

For the asymptotic pure-rail parameters,

\[
 q=d+1=\Theta(\sqrt{k}),\qquad
 M=k-R+q=\frac{k}{2}+\Theta(\sqrt{k}),
\tag{6.1}
\]

so \(M/q\to\infty\). This is neither the Baranyai divisibility case in
general nor the central case \(M=2q+1\). Moreover \(q\) grows with \(M\).
No cited wreath theorem gives the required fixed-core decomposition in
this regime, and Theorem 4.1 shows that such a decomposition is sometimes
impossible even though the **global mixed-core** owner lattice is full.

## 7. Consequence for the positive rail program

The global owner factor must not be organized as independent complete
fixed-core factors. An owner \(A\in{[k]\choose R}\) admits many possible
cores \(C\in{A\choose c}\), and a positive construction must allocate
owners across overlapping core fibres before grouping the assigned petals
into cyclic decks. Core occurrences contribute their period \(N\) to
point degrees, whereas toggle occurrences contribute \(q\); this is how
mixed cores can escape (4.2). The consecutive-period role trades in the
full owner-lattice theorem are the signed shadow of exactly this escape.

Accordingly, the first positive gate is now sharper:

> **Overlapping-core rail allocation and absorption.** Assign each named
> owner to one core, group every resulting petal fibre into legal
> variable-period cyclic decks, and provide a protected conformal absorber
> for the small residual assignment, without reverting to a forbidden
> complete fixed-core factor.

The central wreath theorem validates one boundary model. Baranyai
1-factorization does not close this gate, and the fixed-core divisibility
proves that core mixing is logically essential rather than cosmetic.
